## SG-127 · Heat 5 is not a hard rung, it is a wall — and only you can say whether that is what you wanted

**This needs a design decision from you.** SG-14 built the Heat ladder and
recorded that it was *"graded rather than a cliff"*. That sentence was measured

repaired one, and **it looks false**. There is a cliff. It is at Heat 5.

| Heat | runs | held | average wave |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 240 | 86.7% | 11.77 |
| 3 | 120 | 66.7% | 11.34 |
| 4 | 120 | 77.0% | 11.59 |
| 5 | 120 | **0 of 120 — 0.0%** | **4.02** (sd **0.18**) |

Heats 0 through 4 sit in a shallow twenty-point band. Heat 5 is not further along
that curve — **nothing survives it, and every run dies on wave 4.** An sd of 0.18
on the wave reached means the run does not vary: it is not "hard", it is a fixed
stopping point. For scale, Heat 4 runs reach wave 11.6 and take 222 damage; Heat 5
runs reach wave 4 and take 15, because they are dead before the damage arrives.

**What I am NOT claiming.** Heat 4 reads higher than Heat 3 (77.0% against 66.7%),
which would be a re-ordering of two rungs if it were real. **It is not being
claimed.** Their 95% intervals are 65.1–85.8% and 57.8–74.5% and they overlap;
this sample cannot separate them and I am not going to pretend it can.

**Two caveats, both mine, both stated because the alternative is a curve that
looks complete and is not.** Heats 1 and 2 were **not attempted** — I ran out of
machine time and chose the Article arms over them. And the bot is a competent but
not optimal player: she never repairs, never retreats from a wave she is losing,
and dashes only defensively. A human at Heat 5 may well do better than 0 of 120.
What she does establish is that Heat 5 is a different KIND of rung from the four
below it, not a further step along the same slope.

**The design question, which is yours and not mine.** Heat 5 is SKELETON CREW —
no crew musters and the deck cannons start at half health — and §5's rule is that
the summit is not a power reward, it pays only the record. So a summit that almost

the first one. **Nothing has been tuned and I recommend nothing be tuned until you
have said which you meant** — the honest options are to accept the wall and change
the sentence, or keep the sentence and soften the rung. I have not touched either.

## SG-128 · I told you SG-117's restraint was good discipline. It was good instinct with wrong arithmetic, and the fire fix is measurably real

**Read this one even if you skip the rest, because it corrects something you were
told a few hours ago and it is good news rather than bad.**

When SG-117 fixed the fire immunity it also ran a whole-run before/after and
**declined to claim the result**: damage taken fell 241.2 to 216.7, about
**-10%**, and the row reported it as unresolvable because "two n=120 batches of
identical code differ by 8%, so the floor is the same size as the effect." You
were told that declining to claim it was good discipline. The restraint was
right. **The reason given for it was wrong, and the difference matters to you.**

**The 8% was never a floor.** It is real — two batches of identical code did come
back 8% apart — but it was read as a property of the instrument, when it is a
property of the SAMPLE SIZE. A floor does not shrink when you run more runs;
sampling error does. I bootstrapped it off a pool of 240 real runs: at n=120 this
rig's damage-taken mean has a 95% batch-to-batch range of about **+-6%**, which
is the 8% somebody saw once. At n=240 it is +-4.5%. There is no wall there to
clear.

**So what does SG-117's -10% actually say?** Its own t-statistic was **2.43**,
which is **p=0.015** — and a t-test already accounts for sampling error, so the
floor argument double-counted the noise and threw away the one result of three
that had actually cleared its own bar. Two things are true at once and you should
have both:

- **Believe the sign.** The fire fix made a measurable difference to a whole run,
  not just to the isolated 60-second probe. It is unlikely to be zero.
- **Do not believe the 10%.** n=120 is only about 57% powered to see an effect
  that size (200 runs per arm is the number for 80%), and an underpowered result
  that reaches significance has an inflated magnitude. The true effect is
  probably real and probably smaller than 10%.

**SG-117's conclusion still stands, and on a better argument that was already in
the row**: the balance bot walks out of a fire pool she is standing in, so a
whole-run average barely exercises an exploit that was always available to a
player who *chose* to stand in one. That is the durable reason the run-level
number should not be the headline — and it has nothing to do with floors. The
row rests on it now.

**Nothing shipped wrongly and no tuning value moved.** The three results that
were dismissed under the bad reasoning (SG-117, SG-119's Colossus arc, SG-126's
tick correction) all reach the same verdict on the corrected reasoning. I re-ran
none of them; the correction is to the argument, not to the numbers.

**The one sentence worth carrying:** *this rig can see about a third of a change
and cannot see a twentieth of one at any price.* Detecting a 30% difference costs
23 runs per arm; 10% costs 200; 1% costs about twenty thousand, or 61 hours. The
tool prints that price list under every result now, computed from the spread it
just measured, so nobody has to remember a constant. Anything finer than a third
belongs to a targeted probe and not to a whole-run average.

**Nothing here needs a decision from you.** It is on this list because you were
told something specific and it turned out to be half wrong in your favour.

### 3 · SG-117: standing in fire was a defensive move

`invulnerability_left` is **one global variable**. A fire tick set it to 0.55 s,
four times a second. So a captain in a pool was immune to *everything* — a
boarder's 26-damage swing included — and the pool itself landed one tick in
three.

Measured directly and deterministically (one captain, one pool, a boarder
swinging every 1.5 s for 60 s, stepped at the game's own 1/60):

| standing... | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| **in the fire** | **700 damage** | **1440 damage** |
| clear of the fire | 1014 damage | 1014 damage |
| share of swings that landed, in fire | **51%** | **100%** |
| fire's own contribution | 180 | 426 |

Read the first two rows together, because that is the whole bug: **before the
fix, standing in the fire was 31% SAFER than standing next to it.** After, it
costs 42% more, which is what a hazard is supposed to do. Fire's own damage went
up 2.4x rather than the predicted 3x — the difference is ticks correctly
absorbed by i-frames a *swing* granted, which is the behaviour we kept.

The fix is a `grants_invuln` flag threaded through `damage_player` ->
`take_damage`, defaulting **true** so every discrete hit behaves exactly as it
always has, and passed **false** from periodic sources. I swept for those rather
than assuming: **there is exactly one** — the fire fields. Enemy burn stacks are
a DoT on the *enemy*; the steam taps' half-second tick calls `_damage_circle`,
which iterates enemies and props and never the captain. Four checks pin it,
including the pre-committed `hazard · a fire tick never buys immunity to a
swing` and its converse, `hazard · but a dash still dodges one — the flag
grants, it does not ignore`.

### 4 · Does the fix make the game harder? No — and the run-level number cannot tell you, which is the honest part

You asked for before/after over enough seeds to mean something, and told me not
to compensate by tuning fire down. **I have not touched a tuning value.** But the
run-level answer has to come with its own noise floor, per the rule in STATUS:

| n=120 runs per arm | damage taken | runs held |
|---|---|---|
| before -> after | 241.2 -> 216.7 (**-10%**, t=2.43) | 92% -> 90% (z=-0.45) |
| **two runs of IDENTICAL code** | 241.2 -> 260.1 (**+8%**, t=1.75) | 92% -> 90% (z=-0.45) |

**The floor is the same size as the effect.** Two batches of the same build, same
seeds, differ by 8%; the fix "moves" damage-taken by 10%. So the -10% is *not* a
finding, and I am not going to report it as "the fix made the game easier" — it
is below what this rig can resolve. The hold rate does not move at all, in either
comparison.

The reason the rig cannot see a bug this large is itself worth knowing: **the bot
walks out of fire**, so it barely uses the exploit. The exploit was always
available to a *player who chose to stand in a pool*, and §3's direct probe is the
measurement that actually addresses it. A whole-run bot average was the wrong
instrument for this question, and the floor is what proves that rather than a
feeling.

**What this means for you:** the fix removes a large exploit and does not
measurably change a normal run's difficulty. Nothing needs compensating on the
evidence I have. If it *feels* different when you play it, that is a real signal
and I would rather have your verdict than tune toward my own number.

## SG-130 · The two arms were played by different bots, and fixing that cost the Article its result

The bug, found by SG-128 against its own just-finished measurement: the draft
rule preferred a passive when the draft offered four cards or fewer, and took
cell 0 when it offered more. **The Opening Bid's whole mechanic is that a weapon
draft becomes the 32-cell matrix.** So signing that Article silently swapped the
captain for a different captain — passive share 5.20% in the baseline arm
(114 of 120 runs) against 0.00% in the bid arm (0 of 120) — and nothing measured
across those two arms could be attributed to the vow.

**The rule was not where the ticket said it was.** It was written out inline in
`balance.gd`'s draft loop, not in `tools/bot.gd`. That is the whole reason it
was able to drift: `bot.gd` exists (SG-118) precisely because a policy buried in
a loop is a policy nobody can test, and the draft half had never been moved.

### The policy, and the part of it that is a proof rather than a taste

She takes the best card offered, by a fixed published order over the CARD, and
**the number of cards on the table is not part of the decision.** The order is
the six active shapes in the catalogue's own order, then the two passives —
Field and Pulse — last. Ties, and drafts of cards that are not weapons at all,
go to the first card offered.

Ranking passives last looks arbitrary and is not. There are two passive shapes
out of eight, so **any draft of three or more distinct shapes always contains an
active.** With passives last she takes one only when the game leaves her nothing
else, and widening the offer cannot hand her one. Rank passives above any active
and widening the offer makes a passive strictly *more* likely to be present and
therefore taken — **which re-creates this exact bug in a gentler form.** It is
the only ranking that is both representative and width-invariant. "Always take
cell 0" is also width-invariant, and was rejected as unrepresentative.

### The lemma, which is the reusable part

Actives occupy ranks 0–5 and passives 6–7, so the minimum rank offered is a
passive's only when there is no active on the table at all:

> **`draft_pick` returns a passive if and only if every card offered is a passive.**

It is an identity, not a tendency, and it converts a statistical question into a
structural one. **Any residual gap in passive rate between two arms is a
difference in what the game OFFERED, never in what she PREFERRED** — the exact
opposite of the bug, where the two arms held different preferences.

### Why the obvious proof statistic is the wrong one

The instinct is to show passive share coming out equal across the arms. It does
not, and it should not: **baseline 30.8% against bid 0.0%, a 30.8-point gap at
p < 0.001.** That is correct behaviour. A width-blind policy still draws
differently from different offers — the bid matrix always contains an active, and
a Heat 3 two-card deal sometimes does not.

So the pass condition is structural, and it is a harness check that reads the
policy rather than a statistic that hopes. The statistic that *is* comparable
across arms is **how often she took a passive when an active was on the table**,
which the lemma proves is zero. `balance.gd` counts and prints it now:

    baseline arm   0 of 83 such drafts
    bid arm        0 of 90 such drafts

### Which checks actually bite

Six new checks, harness 915 → 921. Re-implementing the old width-branching rule
turns exactly **three** red, and the headline failure reproduces the bug verbatim:

    FAIL bot · widening a draft does not change the card she takes
             AURA at 4 cards, CONE at 32
    FAIL bot · and she takes the best card offered, not the first cell
    FAIL bot · she takes a passive only when the draft leaves her nothing else

**The other three are guards or controls and are not evidence the bug is fixed.**
The draft-order guard only fails if the shape table and the bot's order drift
apart — demonstrated by promoting AURA to the front, which turns it red. The

# SG-129 — THE PRE-SPECIFICATION, WRITTEN BEFORE THE DATA EXISTED

## The test, copied from the row and not redesigned

One statistic: **damage taken**. One test: **OLS slope of that statistic against
run index**. One threshold: **p < 0.05**. **n = 480 in a single invocation at
Heat 0.** Control: **a second set of 480 runs split into four 120-run
processes.** If the slope is real it appears in the first and not the second.

Nothing else will be computed. No wave-reached, no held-rate, no first-third-vs-
last-third split, no block means, no Stouffer combination, no second pool. The
row was withdrawn once for exactly that and it will not be re-mined here.

## The five ambiguities, resolved IN WRITING before the data was generated

The row is precise but not complete. Every gap is closed here, in advance,
rather than being closed by whichever choice the data later rewarded.

1. **What "run index" means.** The 0-based order of EXECUTION within the
   invocation, which is the order `balance.gd` prints and emits: its loop is
   `for rep in reps: for i in count`, so index = rep * 6 + seed_i. This is the
   order in the `SAMPLES ... taken=` list, so the index is read off the tool's
   own output and not reconstructed.

2. **What "a second invocation split into four 120-run processes" means.** An
   invocation cannot literally be split into processes, so the phrase is read
   the only way that makes the contrast meaningful: **four separate `balance.gd`
   processes of 120 runs each, launched sequentially, their `taken` lists
   concatenated in launch order into one 480-long series indexed 0..479, and the
   IDENTICAL OLS slope computed on it.** The control therefore differs from the
   primary in exactly one respect — process boundaries at runs 120, 240 and 360
   — which is the thing the hypothesis is about.

3. **Sidedness.** Two-sided. The suspected direction is negative, but a
   one-sided test chosen after a direction was observed in an earlier pool is
   the same error the row withdrew itself for.

4. **Configuration.** Heat 0, no Article, `SKYGEAR_TEMPO_FLAT` unset, 
   `SKYGEAR_PASSIVE_PROBE` unset, count = 6 seeds. Primary: `reps = 80`
   (6 x 80 = 480). Control: four processes at `reps = 20` (6 x 20 = 120).
   Both arms run in a clean worktree extracted from HEAD, sequentially, with no
   other heavy job on the machine — because a control whose processes race the
   primary for CPU is measuring the scheduler.

5. **The decision rule, all four cells fixed in advance** — because "it appears
   in the first and not the second" names two of the four outcomes and the other
   two have to mean something too:

   | primary | control | verdict |
   |---|---|---|
   | p < 0.05 | p >= 0.05 | **WITHIN-INVOCATION DRIFT ESTABLISHED.** Sequential arms inside one invocation are confounded by run order. |
   | p < 0.05 | p < 0.05 | **NOT established as a within-invocation effect.** A trend that survives process boundaries is not process state; it is something the two arms share (machine, wall clock, thermal) and is reported as that. |
   | p >= 0.05 | p >= 0.05 | **NOT ESTABLISHED**, at a power stated below. |
   | p >= 0.05 | p < 0.05 | Anomalous. Reported as anomalous, claimed as nothing. |

## The price list applied FIRST, as the task requires

`bal_stat.gd`'s functions price a DIFFERENCE OF TWO MEANS, and this test is a
slope, so the arithmetic is the same family but not the same formula, and it is
written out here rather than borrowed:

For run indices 0..n-1 the sum of squared deviations is n(n^2-1)/12, so
SE(slope) = sd / sqrt(n(n^2-1)/12). At n = 480 that denominator is 3035.8.
Taking sd = 75 (the 240-run Heat 0 pool's across-run sd, CV 0.36 on a mean near
210) gives **SE = 0.0247 damage per run**, and an 80%-power two-sided threshold
of 2.8 x SE = **0.069 damage per run**.

**So n = 480 can establish a drift of 0.069/run or steeper — which is a total
change of 33 damage across the invocation, about 16% of the mean.** It cannot
establish anything shallower.

**That is enough, and this is why the test is worth running rather than
declining.** The two slopes that made SG-128 suspicious were -0.12/run (Heat 0)
and -0.23/run (Heat 3). Against SE = 0.0247 those are t = 4.9 and t = 9.3. If
the drift that was suggested is real at anything like the size it was suggested
at, n = 480 sees it with room to spare. A drift far shallower than 0.069/run
would not invalidate a within-invocation A/B in any practical way, so the n that
is affordable and the n that is worth caring about are the same n here. This is
the case where "not attempted, here is the price" is NOT the right answer.

## And this is a STRONG not-established, not a shrug — which is the part worth reading

A null result is only worth as much as the power behind it, and this one has the
power, because the effect that raised the suspicion is *excluded* rather than
merely un-confirmed:

* The realized SE on the primary slope is **0.0176/run**, so the 95% interval on

* The slopes that made SG-128 suspicious were **-0.12/run** (Heat 0) and

  about 6 SEs from the estimate.
* At 80% power this n reaches a slope of **0.049/run**, an end-to-end drift of
  **19.8% of the mean**. The fitted end-to-end change is **-6.9%**, and its
  interval spans zero.

Quoting the interval is not a second test; it is the same OLS fit with its
uncertainty attached, which is what SG-128 exists to insist on.

**And the control's slope points the OTHER WAY (+0.025/run against the primary's

what "no trend plus noise" looks like from the inside. If run order were doing
something, the sign would at least be stable.

## What this buys, stated narrowly

**Arms run sequentially inside a single `balance.gd` invocation are NOT
confounded by run order at any magnitude this rig could act on.** The
within-invocation A/B shape is sound. This protects future work; it re-opens
nothing, because as SG-129 already noted, almost every comparison in the ledger
used separate processes and was immune either way.

**What it does NOT say:** that the slope is exactly zero. A drift shallower than
~0.05/run (under 20% end to end) is still unresolved and would cost roughly
n = 1000 in one invocation to see at half that size, since SE(slope) falls as
n^-1.5. Nobody should care at that size, which is why the test stops here.

## THE TOOL, so this is re-runnable rather than re-argued

`tools/bal_drift.gd` (hub: `drift`) reads saved `balance.gd` logs, concatenates

## What the fix is, and why it is not the renderer restructure the row feared

The row expected the aim dashes to need their own vertex buffer, their own
budget and their own build path — a real restructure, which is why it was filed
rather than done. **It does not.** Every ribbon in this renderer is written by
ONE function (`_ribbon()`), into ONE scratch buffer, flushed ONCE per frame by
`_ribbons_end()`. So the dashes do not need a second writer; **they only need a
second FLUSH.**

`_aim_dash_ribbon()` records the vertex range it just wrote
(`_dash_ranges.append(Vector2i(from, to))`), and `_ribbons_end()` sends those
ranges to a second `MeshInstance3D` that **shares the material instance** — so
the dashes cannot drift in appearance from what they looked like before. On any
frame with no ranged windup, `_dash_ranges` is empty and the function takes a
fast path that is the old code byte for byte.

**Nothing else moves.** `_ribbon()`, `RIBBON_VERTS`, `_ribbon_verts`,
`_ribbon_peak`, `_ribbon_dropped` and every existing harness assertion that reads
them are untouched — which is the whole reason this turned out cheap. The scoping
pass expected to have to edit those assertions; it did not.

`SkyGearRune.hide()` then takes the new node by the same `visible` idiom it
already uses for decals — **but only on frames where `_dash_ranges` is non-empty.**
That detail is load-bearing: the node exists on every deck while the dashes exist
only during a windup, so hiding it unconditionally would make `decals` non-zero on
a deck with no telegraph posed, destroying the "you posed nothing" guard AND
breaking `rune_probe.gd`'s falsifiability pose (a lit brazier, no windup, mask
must come back EMPTY).

### SG-149 — a green run is not the same as a clean run

A freshly extracted worktree of HEAD printed
`SCRIPT ERROR: Invalid access to property or key 'cooldown_left' on a base
object of type 'Dictionary'` **fifteen times**, reported **997/997 checks
passed**, and **exited 0**. It had presumably been doing that for a long time.

**The dictionary was the small half, and it was neither candidate on the row.**
It is three fixtures in the harness itself — the SG-126 passive-tick checks —
each of which wrote a skill dictionary by hand:
`{shape, element, mods, passive_timer}`. That is every field `_update_passives`
reads and none of the ones it does not. Each rig is then set to `State.PLAY`
**and it is in the tree**, so its real `_process` runs beside the check:
`_update_cooldowns` (`game.gd:3038`) walked those skills, and so did the HUD's
`_draw` -> `_draw_game_hud` (`hud.gd:2506`). Five rigs x one `_process` = 5,
five rigs x two draws = 10. Exactly the fifteen. Isolated by instrumenting both
sites to print `skill.keys()` rather than by guessing at it.

**Is it cosmetic, or is a cooldown silently not ticking? Cosmetic — and here is
why that is not a shrug.** `SkyGearData.make_skill` (`game_data.gd:465`) is the
only producer of a skill dictionary in the game and it always carries
`cooldown_left`. The one place shipped code puts a keyless `{}` into `skills` is
`_slot_skill` (`game.gd:2181`), and those placeholders are removed by
`_trim_empty_slots` inside the same synchronous call, with no `await` between,
so no frame can ever observe them. **No cooldown is failing to tick for anybody
playing the game.**

**But the mechanism is not cosmetic, and it is why this was worth the hour.**
A GDScript runtime error is not an exception. It prints, **it abandons the rest
of the function it was raised in**, and control returns to the caller as though
the call had completed. Verified directly rather than assumed: a `d.missing` on
line N, the line after it never printed, the caller's next line did. So in each
of those five rigs `_update_cooldowns` returned at its first statement and
`_draw_game_hud` stopped painting mid-HUD, silently, and the checks passed
anyway. With one skill in the fixture nothing measurable was lost. With two, the
second one's cooldown would have stopped ticking and the check would still have
been green.

**The fix is the fixture, not a `.get()` default.** `_passive_fixture()` builds
through `make_skill`, so it cannot drift from the schema again. `game.gd` and
`hud.gd` keep their dot access on purpose and are byte-unchanged: a skill
without `cooldown_left` is a bug in either direction, and a silent
`.get("cooldown_left", 0.0)` would have deleted the only signal that found this.
That would have been the third failure mode committed as a fix.

### And the half that actually matters: a run that raises can no longer report green

**It IS detectable from inside.** Godot 4.7 exposes `Logger` with `_log_error`
and `OS.add_logger`, so the run reads its own error log — no wrapper, no log

the hub. `ErrorWatch` installs in `_initialize`, before the first deferred call,
so the window it watches is the whole run, and it counts by `ERROR_TYPE_SCRIPT`
against `ERROR_TYPE_ERROR`.

Two checks, last in `_run` on purpose because they are a verdict on everything
above them, and neither can raise — the index into the error list is guarded by
the same `is_empty()` that decides the verdict.

- **`harness · the run raised no script errors`** — the witness.
- **`harness · and no more engine errors than the ones already written down`** —
